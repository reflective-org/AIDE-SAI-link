The emulator tracks the JCM dycore it learned from: within 1.5–2.7 K and 6–9 m/s, with no mean bias. Most of its distance from CESM and ERA5 is already present in the dycore runs themselves, so it comes from the dycore's climate, not from emulation:

  • a warm tropical tropopause and upper stratosphere;
  • subtropical jets near 200 hPa that are present but much weaker than in CESM and ERA5 (about half the ERA5 peak near 30°N in DJF) in all three JCM-based fields;
  • an equatorial upper-stratospheric westerly the references don't have.

Age of air

An age tracer in a transport-only run with the campaign's advection and no chemistry. Age is reset to zero on the 143 hPa level between 10°S and 10°N and grows by the time step everywhere else. The 1997 meteorology is cycled for 12 years, once on the emulator winds and once on CESM 1997 winds as a control.

The campaign's mass fixer was switched off for this tracer. The global fixer (ADV_SL_FIXER=global) rescales each whole level every step. That's fine for a mass tracer, but for a clock tracer the error grows with elapsed time. Through the campaign operator, age rose until about year 8 and then fell (3.5 → 2.0 yr at 10 hPa, equator), which is impossible under a repeating circulation. In a 30-day test with the fixer on, 85–92 % of cells were older than the elapsed time; with it off, about 1 %, all next to the source. Everything else matches the campaign transport.

Age of air, 10 years of 1997–1998 winds: surface reset vs 150 hPa reset

Same tracer setup (fixer off), now cycling the two emulator years 1997–1998 five times (10 model years) on the full column: the 24 aerosol-model levels plus 21 fixed levels from 160 to 1000 hPa, with the wind set to zero below the local surface. Two clocks run side by side:

  • Surface reset: age = 0 at the ground.
  • 150 hPa reset: age = 0 everywhere at p ≥ 150 hPa.
  • The published 143 hPa tropical-band setup was rerun on the same two-year winds for reference (band).

Figures use the mean of the last 1997–1998 cycle. Drift over the last cycle, 100–5 hPa mean: surface +0.043, 150 hPa +0.015, band +0.056 yr/yr. That is close to equilibrium; the remaining rise is small next to the differences below.

  • The surface-reset age is about 0.93–0.95 yr older than the 150 hPa age throughout the tropical stratosphere. That is the time air takes on these winds to get from the ground to 150 hPa. It is far too long: CLaMS has under 0.1 yr at the tropical 150 hPa. The emulator meteorology has resolved winds only, with no convective mass flux, so surface air reaches the upper troposphere by slow large-scale ascent.
  • Its good overall score against CLaMS (RMS 0.63 yr) is partly compensation. It is up to ~1 yr too old at 70–120 hPa in the tropics and subtropics, and about 1 yr too young at 30–100 hPa in midlatitudes.
  • Above the entry level the circulation is too fast or too weakly mixed: the 150 hPa age is 0.8–1.2 yr younger than WACCM6 and shifted CLaMS. The band clock looks closer (mean bias +0.01 yr against WACCM6) only because it is reset in the tropics alone, so extratropical air entering below 143 hPa starts old.

Microphysics TOMAS emulated vs numerical

10,000 grid cells, each run as a closed box for 24 h (4 × 6 h) under TOMAS numerical and the TOMAS emulator: 5,000 background cells from the control run and 5,000 SAI cells from inside the plume, sampled at 40 times across the year. Each cell keeps its own temperature, humidity and pressure; OH follows the day–night cycle. CARMA and MAM4 are left out here.

The emulator is run exactly as deployed in the linked model: physics routing for the stiffest cells, the one-year-spec knobs, quiet-cell triage and the chemistry closure. Routing ranks cells over the whole grid, so the emulator runs on every cell of each sampled frame and the sampled cells are then compared.

How the shortwave correction compares with the raw and earlier estimates

ΔT: stratospheric aerosol heating

With no feedback the temperature never responds, so ΔT is shown as the extra heating rate the aerosol causes: injection minus control.

Heating by latitude and season

  • Longwave heating follows the aerosol and has little seasonal cycle of its own: it grows steadily in the two plume belts (30–60° and 0–30° in each hemisphere) as aerosol builds up. Over the polar caps it is small, and over Antarctica it turns negative from about day 310 to the end of the year (down to −10 mK/day).
  • Shortwave heating is strongly seasonal and changes sign with latitude. It is negative at high latitudes in each hemisphere's summer, down to −15 to −20 mK/day: the aerosol reflects sunlight that the gases below would otherwise absorb. It is slightly positive (+2 to +7 mK/day) in the subtropics. The polar shortwave signal switches off in polar night, which gives the curved edges in the top row.
  • Net heating is therefore seasonal only at high latitudes: the polar caps are net cooled in summer (Arctic about −18 mK/day around day 140, Antarctic about −25 mK/day in late December), while the 30°N and 30°S belts warm steadily, up to 40–47 mK/day.
  • MAM4 follows TOMAS closely in longwave; its summer polar shortwave cooling is weaker, so its high-latitude net cooling is smaller.
  • Emulator minus numerical is largest along the plume belts late in the year (up to about 1 mK/day, a few percent), the same place its particles are smaller.

Size distribution per grid cell

Column size statistics over stratospheric levels: column number per bin, lognormal fit by moments for the geometric mean diameter Dg and σg, mass-weighted diameter, and effective diameter dry and wet (Tabazadeh water uptake, the model's own code). Each TOMAS column holds two populations, a numerous mode below 10 nm and a mass-carrying mode at 300–800 nm, so Dg and σg are compromise values. MAM4 stores fixed-width lognormal modes (σg 1.8/1.6/1.8), spread here over the TOMAS bins, so its Dg and σg are not like-for-like with TOMAS; compare the mass-weighted and effective diameters instead.
